Spectral

Combining, shifting and reshaping spectra: Warps' cross-modulation, Hilbert transform and vocoder, Plaits' vowel filter and FM operators, and Rings' onset detector.

Xmod

#include "pt/spectral/xmod.h" · from Warps

Warps' algorithms for combining two signals, a modulator and a carrier, into one. They are plain per-sample functions, so they cost almost nothing.

AlgorithmWhat it doesparameter
XFADEEqual-power crossfade.Position: 0 carrier, 1 modulator.
FOLDSum (plus a little product) through a wavefolder measured from analog circuitry.Drive.
ANALOG_RINGRing modulation through a diode-ring model: warm, with leakage and grit.Drive; the output can exceed ±1 when driven hard.
DIGITAL_RINGMultiplication, soft-clipped: the clean, clangy ring mod.Drive.
XORBoth inputs as 16-bit integers, XORed: harsh digital fizz.Blend from plain sum to XOR.
COMPARATORChooses between the inputs by comparing them.Mode: minimum, threshold, windowed, rectified.
NOPThe modulator alone.

Calls. All are static: Xmod::process(algorithm, modulator, carrier, parameter) runs one algorithm, and Xmod::morph(position, modulator, carrier, parameter) sweeps through all of them, crossfading between neighbours, like Warps' big knob. Each algorithm can also be called directly, for example Xmod::digitalRing(a, b, p).

Aliasing. These are nonlinear. Warps ran them 6× oversampled; at the host rate, bright inputs alias. Feed them smooth signals, or oversample with Rack's dsp::Upsampler and dsp::Decimator.

// A Warps-style cross-modulator: one knob through all the algorithms
float mod = inputs[MODULATOR].getVoltage() / 5.f;
float car = inputs[CARRIER].getVoltage() / 5.f;
float out = pt::Xmod::morph(params[ALGORITHM].getValue(), mod, car, params[TIMBRE].getValue());
outputs[OUT].setVoltage(5.f * out);
// Or a plain ring modulator with a drive knob
float ring = pt::Xmod::process(pt::Xmod::ANALOG_RING, mod, car, params[DRIVE].getValue());

Hilbert

#include "pt/filter/hilbert.h" · from Warps

Splits a signal into two copies 90° apart at every frequency from 10 Hz to 20 kHz, an in-phase signal I and a quadrature signal Q. This is the core of a frequency shifter, also called a Bode or single-sideband modulator: unlike a pitch shifter, it moves every partial by the same number of Hz, turning harmonic sounds inharmonic. sqrt(I² + Q²) also gives a smooth amplitude envelope.

How it works. Two chains of all-pass filters, which change phase but not level, are designed so their phases stay 90° apart (within about 1°). The design (an elliptic half-band filter split into all-passes, as in Warps) is computed for the actual sample rate; at 96 kHz it reproduces Warps' table exactly.

CallEffect
init(sampleRate)Designs the filters.
process(in)Returns I; q() then holds Q.
Hilbert::designPoles(sr, coefficients)The design on its own (17 coefficients).
// Frequency shifter: shift every partial up (or down) by `shift` Hz
pt::Hilbert hilbert;
float phase = 0.f;   // members; onSampleRateChange: hilbert.init(e.sampleRate);

float shift = 500.f * (2.f * params[SHIFT].getValue() - 1.f);   // -500..+500 Hz
phase += shift * args.sampleTime;
phase -= std::floor(phase);
float i = hilbert.process(inputs[IN].getVoltage() / 5.f);
float q = hilbert.q();
float c = std::cos(2.f * M_PI * phase), s = std::sin(2.f * M_PI * phase);
outputs[UP].setVoltage(5.f * (i * c - q * s));     // shifted up
outputs[DOWN].setVoltage(5.f * (i * c + q * s));   // shifted down

VowelFilter

#include "pt/filter/vowel.h" · from Plaits (simple speech synthesiser)

A "talking" filter: five resonant band-passes tuned to the formants of a human voice, morphing across five vowels and five voice types. Put any bright source through it (a pulse train, a saw, noise) and it speaks.

How it works. A vowel is mostly defined by where the mouth and throat resonate, at a handful of frequencies called formants. A table holds the five formants (frequency and level) for each vowel and voice type; the controls blend between entries and retune the filters.

CallEffect
setVowel(x)0..1, through Plaits' five vowels (roughly a, e, i, o, u).
setVoice(x)0..1, voice type from low (bass) to high (child).
setShift(semitones)Moves all formants up or down: a smaller or bigger "head".
process(in)The input through the formants. The resonances are strong; feed about ±0.2.
// A singing pulse: Plaits drives this filter with a band-limited pulse train
pt::BasicOscillator pulse;
pt::VowelFilter vowel;
MyModule() { pulse.setShape(pt::BasicOscillator::IMPULSE_TRAIN); }
// onSampleRateChange: pulse.init(sr); vowel.init(sr);

pulse.setFrequency(pt::voltToHz(inputs[VOCT].getVoltage()));
vowel.setVowel(pt::clamp(params[VOWEL].getValue() + inputs[VOWEL_CV].getVoltage() / 10.f, 0.f, 1.f));
vowel.setVoice(params[VOICE].getValue());
outputs[OUT].setVoltage(5.f * vowel.process(0.2f * pulse.process()));

Vocoder

#include "pt/spectral/vocoder.h" · from Warps

Imposes the spectral envelope of one sound (the modulator, usually a voice) on another (the carrier, usually a rich synth tone): the classic talking synth.

How it works.

  1. Both signals are split into 20 bands a third of an octave apart (from about 87 Hz to 7 kHz).
  2. An envelope follower measures the modulator in each band.
  3. Each carrier band is scaled by that level, and the bands are summed.
  4. Moving formantShift away from 0.5 crossfades to the modulator's band levels shifted up or down the bank, for "chipmunk" and "giant" effects.
  5. A limiter keeps the output in range.

Difference from Warps. Warps ran its low and middle bands at reduced rates with filters designed offline for 96 kHz. Here every band runs at the host rate with filters designed at run time, so it works at any sample rate, at a higher CPU cost of about 160 filter sections per sample.

CallEffect
setRelease(x)0..1. Envelope speed, from articulate to smeared. At 1 the envelopes freeze, holding the spectrum.
setFormantShift(x)0..1. 0.5 is normal vocoding; lower or higher shifts the formants down or up (about ±2 octaves at the extremes).
process(modulator, carrier)Returns the vocoded signal.
pt::Vocoder vocoder;   // onSampleRateChange: vocoder.init(e.sampleRate);

vocoder.setRelease(params[RELEASE].getValue());
vocoder.setFormantShift(params[SHIFT].getValue());
float voice = inputs[MODULATOR].getVoltage() / 5.f;
float synth = inputs[CARRIER].getVoltage() / 5.f;   // a saw or chord works best
outputs[OUT].setVoltage(5.f * vocoder.process(voice, synth));

FmOperators

#include "pt/spectral/fm_operators.h" · from Plaits (FM engine)

A 4- or 6-operator FM voice in the style of Yamaha's DX synths, with all 8 four-operator (DX100) or 32 six-operator (DX7) algorithms. There are no patches or envelopes: you set each operator's frequency ratio and level, drive the levels from your own envelopes, and choose an algorithm.

How it works. Each operator is a sine whose phase can be pushed by other operators' outputs ("FM", strictly phase modulation). A modulator's level sets the modulation index, which controls how many harmonics appear. The algorithm decides which operators modulate which and which are heard. One operator per algorithm can feed back into itself or its chain, from smooth to noisy. Operators are numbered as on the DX synths: operator 1 is usually a carrier.

CallEffect
init(sampleRate, numOperators = 6)4 or 6 operators.
setAlgorithm(n)1..32 (6 operators) or 1..8 (4), numbered as on the synths.
setFrequency(hz)Base pitch.
setRatio(op, r), setFixedFrequency(op, hz)Operator pitch as a ratio of the base, or fixed in Hz.
setLevel(op, x)Carriers: loudness 0..1. Modulators: index 0..4; about 1 is bright and brassy.
setFeedback(x)0..1, the DX7's 0..7.
reset()Restart all phases; call on note-on for a consistent attack.
// An electric piano-ish 2-operator patch on DX7 algorithm 5, with envelopes
pt::FmOperators fm;
pt::Envelope carrierEnv, modulatorEnv;
dsp::SchmittTrigger gateTrig;
// onSampleRateChange: fm.init(sr, 6); carrierEnv.init(sr); modulatorEnv.init(sr);
//   fm.setAlgorithm(5); fm.setRatio(1, 1.f); fm.setRatio(2, 14.f);
//   carrierEnv.setAd(0.002f, 1.5f); modulatorEnv.setAd(0.001f, 0.3f);

bool gate = inputs[GATE].getVoltage() >= 1.f;
if (gateTrig.process(inputs[GATE].getVoltage(), 0.1f, 1.f)) fm.reset();
fm.setFrequency(pt::voltToHz(inputs[VOCT].getVoltage()));
fm.setLevel(1, 0.8f * carrierEnv.process(gate));                       // carrier
fm.setLevel(2, params[TINE].getValue() * 2.f * modulatorEnv.process(gate));   // the "tine"
outputs[OUT].setVoltage(5.f * fm.process());

OnsetDetector

#include "pt/analysis/onset_detector.h" · from Rings

Detects when a new note or hit starts in audio. Rings uses it to strum its internal exciter from the audio input; use it to trigger envelopes, drums or sequencers from a guitar, a drum loop or a voice.

How it works.

  1. Automatic gain control evens out the level.
  2. The signal is split into three bands and their energy tracked.
  3. An onset fires when the sum of the bands' energy increases jumps well above its recent average (a z-score test), the energy is above the level left by the last onset, and the minimum interval has passed.

Analysis runs every 0.5 ms, so detection lags by up to that.

CallEffect
init(sampleRate, minInterval = 0.05)Shortest time between onsets, in seconds. Rings used 0.01, which can register a sharp attack several times.
process(in)Returns true on the sample an onset is detected; onset() repeats it.
// Audio to trigger: a 1 ms pulse on each detected onset
pt::OnsetDetector onsets;      // onSampleRateChange: onsets.init(e.sampleRate);
dsp::PulseGenerator pulse;

if (onsets.process(inputs[IN].getVoltage() / 5.f)) pulse.trigger(1e-3f);
outputs[TRIG].setVoltage(pulse.process(args.sampleTime) ? 10.f : 0.f);
